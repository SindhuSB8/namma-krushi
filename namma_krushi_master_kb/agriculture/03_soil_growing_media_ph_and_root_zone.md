# Soil, growing media, pH and root-zone management

Tomato performs best in a well-drained root zone with good aeration and enough organic matter or a well-managed soilless substrate. TNAU describes well-drained loamy soil rich in organic matter as suitable and gives a soil pH range around 6.5–7.5 for field production, while recent ICAR protected-tomato material describes sandy loam around pH 6.0–7.0 as ideal. These ranges overlap around slightly acidic to near-neutral conditions. Local soil and water testing should guide exact correction.

## Why drainage matters

Tomato roots need both water and oxygen. Saturated beds displace air, weaken roots and encourage soil-borne diseases. A farmer should not interpret “soil moisture high” as “better.” The useful target is adequate and stable moisture without prolonged saturation.

## Soil versus soilless media

Soil-grown polyhouses are simpler but can accumulate soil-borne pathogens and salts when poorly managed. Soilless production using media such as cocopeat can improve root-zone control but requires careful irrigation, nutrient solution and drainage management. A media system is not automatically safer; poor sanitation or excessive nutrient concentration can still damage roots.

## pH and nutrient availability

Very acidic or alkaline conditions can make some nutrients less available even when fertilizer is present. If nutrient deficiency symptoms persist despite feeding, check pH, irrigation water quality, drainage and root health rather than repeatedly increasing fertilizer.

## Useful farmer observations

Ask whether water drains quickly, whether the bed smells anaerobic, whether roots are white/cream or brown, whether salt crust is visible, and whether symptoms are uniform across an irrigation line. These clues help distinguish a whole-house nutrient or irrigation issue from an isolated disease problem.
