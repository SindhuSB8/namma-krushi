# Physiological disorders

Physiological disorders are non-infectious problems caused by the interaction of water, nutrients, temperature, light and plant growth. They may resemble disease but are not necessarily caused by a pathogen.

## Blossom-end rot

Blossom-end rot appears as a sunken dark lesion at the blossom end of fruit and is strongly associated with inadequate calcium reaching developing fruit. This can happen even when soil calcium is present because irregular water supply, root stress or rapid growth disrupts calcium transport. Stabilize irrigation and investigate root-zone conditions before assuming that simply adding calcium will solve the problem.

## Fruit cracking

Cracking is commonly associated with sudden changes in water availability and rapid fruit expansion. Keeping soil moisture more uniform and avoiding severe dry-wet cycles helps reduce risk. Variety and weather also influence susceptibility.

## Sunscald

Fruit exposed suddenly to intense sunlight after heavy pruning or leaf loss can develop pale or damaged areas. Maintain adequate foliage around fruit while still managing canopy airflow.

## Puffiness and poor filling

Poor pollination, nutrition imbalance and environmental stress can produce fruit with poor internal filling. Check flowering conditions and overall plant balance.

## Important distinction

If a lesion spreads between plants, produces characteristic leaf spots, vascular browning, fungal growth, bacterial ooze or is associated with insect vectors, consider an infectious disease rather than a purely physiological disorder.
